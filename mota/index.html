Read this first: the lens this report uses

Every finding below follows from these readings. If one is wrong, the answer changes; corrections go in the form at the end.

  • MOTA's size. A few hundred users at most, both women who have not had cancer and women who have been diagnosed. Every ask is sized to that figure, not to the population.
  • MOTA's legal status. A for-profit company, not a nonprofit. Most drug company grants and many sponsorships are open only to nonprofits.
  • The goal. Drug company sponsorship of MOTA.
  • Who is in scope. Drug and drug discovery companies. Diagnostics companies, nonprofits and academic centers were not ranked as funders.
  • People. Women with an inherited (germline) BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation, in the United States. Mutations found only in a tumor are out of scope; men are saved for later.

Summary

Drug companies were not found paying a company of MOTA’s size for its audience, and most of their patient-program money goes to nonprofits (C062, C068, C156, C205, C207). The realistic first ask is a single paid proof of concept: one unbranded community gathering for women with an inherited BRCA mutation, sponsored by a company whose rules allow for-profit organizers (C220, C222). Daiichi Sankyo accepts sponsorship requests from for-profit organizations for non-educational community events, in exchange for being acknowledged as a sponsor (C220). Pfizer’s sponsorship definition covers for-profit organizers, but Pfizer’s request checklist asks for nonprofit status (C222). Merck paid nonprofits $5,000 to $40,000 for comparable support meetings and hereditary cancer education (C223). A delivered gathering, with attendance and feedback, is the evidence MOTA needs before any larger ask.

Whom to contact. Daiichi Sankyo’s US Corporate and Community Sponsorships, through its online request, at least 60 days before the event (C220, C221). No named person was found.

Why Daiichi Sankyo. Of the companies whose sponsorship rules were read, Daiichi Sankyo’s are the only ones found that name for-profit organizations as eligible without conditions (C220, C222, C225). It already supports FORCE and Sharsheret, and its current oncology programs include ovarian and triple-negative breast cancer (C055, C126, C132). It is not in the ranked lists, which scored companies on BRCA-specific spending; it enters here on its sponsorship rules.

The risk MOTA most needs to know. Daiichi Sankyo’s for-profit route covers community events, not medical education, so the gathering has to be planned as a community event that acknowledges the sponsor and mentions no product. Daiichi Sankyo publishes no typical amount, and no drug company payment to a for-profit patient community for an event was found (C220, C226).

Findings.

  1. About 435,000 to 1.1 million US women and girls carry an inherited BRCA variant, and at least about 9,500 carriers are diagnosed each year (C173, C181, C219); these bound the market and are not MOTA’s reach.
  2. No large drug company was found targeting carriers without cancer; the money found follows diagnosis (C025, C142, C143).
  3. Grants at AstraZeneca, Gilead, Merck, Genentech, GSK, and Moderna are nonprofit-only, and so are event sponsorships at Merck, Gilead, and Novartis (C062, C066, C068, C071, C149, C156, C225).
  4. Outside drug companies, MOTA may be eligible for an NIH SBIR Phase I grant of about $150,000 to $400,000, to confirm (C188, C191).
  5. Considered and set aside: paid patient-insight sessions, by a decision supplied for this report (C195); and a Pfizer content license, because nothing shows MOTA’s content is different from what Pfizer can get elsewhere, and the precedent company is in administration (C210, C218).

Main uncertainties. Every amount cited went to a nonprofit; no payment to a for-profit organizer was found (C226).
